What happens when integers are studied not only as quantities, but as elements of a generative mathematical structure?

Recent UNNS research has produced several remarkable answers. Consecutive integers, however large, are separated by at most six steps in a particular arithmetic graph. A deterministic selection rule generates an endlessly nonrepeating sequence governed by an exact irrational constant. And the same arithmetic foundation supports finite graph structures whose symbolic entropy grows without bound.

Most strikingly, one exact equation now connects three seemingly different mathematical objects: the number of divisors of an integer, the topology of its associated graph, and the entropy of its possible histories.

τ(x) − 1  =  β1(F(x))  =  e3h − e2h + eh

Together, these results reveal a mathematical landscape in which arithmetic, geometry, and dynamics become different expressions of generative structure. This article follows that landscape from its foundation to its culminating identity. Every constant below is derived, not fitted, and every claim is tied to one of four standalone mathematical manuscripts and an extended synthesis.

Section 1

A Hidden Geometry of Numbers

Start with a rule so simple it fits on one line. For positive integers M and N,

M ⊥ N = M · (N + 1)² / N

is a whole number exactly when N divides M. Write M = kN and the result is k(N + 1)². Each legal step therefore draws a connection between two integers, kN and k(N + 1)². Allow those connections to be walked in either direction and the integers become the vertices of a single connected graph.

That graph has a geometry of its own, and it does not resemble the number line. Its distances obey three sharp laws:

Consecutive integers
≤ 6
sup d(n, n+1) = 6
An integer and its double
≤ 5
sup d(n, 2n) = 5
Consecutive squares
≤ 5
sup d(n², (n+1)²) = 5

These are not estimates. They are sharp suprema: the bounds are attained. The integers 1 and 2 are exactly five steps apart, and the consecutive-square bound is reached at 23² and 24². The graph is triangle-free with girth four (attributed to the corrected U3-COR source) and odd girth five, and every vertex has degree τ(x) + #{d ≥ 2 : d² divides x}, so primes have degree two and no vertex has degree three.

Why it matters

Numerical closeness does not determine structural distance. Two integers a billion apart can be a few steps apart in the graph, and the metric is built from divisibility, not subtraction. The reversible graph also permits a path to climb to a clever intermediate value and descend again, which a forward-only generation rule never could. Source: Geometry and Metric Structure of the UNNS Arithmetic Graph, v1.4.

Section 2

The Arithmetic Mechanism Behind the Structure

Why should such different structures come from one rule? Because the rule has an elegant algebraic shadow. Look at the prime factorization of a number before and after a step. Multiplying by (N + 1)²/N adds twice the exponents of N + 1 and subtracts the exponents of N. For every prime p:

vp(TN(x)) = vp(x) + 2·vp(N + 1) − vp(N)
the valuation law

Multiplication has become addition on a lattice of prime exponents. A legal move is a fixed integer translation, and legality is a set of simple threshold conditions: the exponent of each prime in x must be at least its exponent in N. Everything that follows, the graph, the selectors, the entropy family, is built on this one identity.

Two consequences are worth stating plainly. First, the factor (N + 1)²/N is at least 9/2 for N ≥ 2, so every forward step grows the number and forward histories can never loop. Second, the same lattice view explains which words of moves can be run from a given start: a finite word is executable from x0 exactly when a single least-common-multiple of denominators divides x0.

Nothing here yet says which move to make. That choice, made automatically from the state of the number alone, is where order without repetition appears.

Section 3

Order Without Repetition

Give the system a rule for choosing: at every step, fire the prime whose prime-power part of the number is largest. Call this selector MAXPP. It reads only the current number. Nothing is random and nothing is added from outside.

Higher-prime firings cease; two active branches remain

A short argument shows that primes 5 and above can fire only finitely many times in total (not that any particular primes vanish from the number): each such firing strictly lowers a well-founded potential built from the exponents of the larger primes. After a finite transient, only branches 2 and 3 remain, and the active dynamics lives on two coordinates with translations (−1, +2) and (+4, −1).

An exact irrational rotation

Compare the two surviving prime-power parts with the single quantity f = v2 ln 2 − v3 ln 3. Branch 2 shifts f by −ln 18, branch 3 by +ln 48, and f is eventually confined to an interval of length ln 864. Both moves are the same rotation by ln 48 on a circle of circumference ln 864. The rotation number is

θ = ln 48 / ln 864 = 0.5725296678…
irrational, because 48q = 864p has no solution in positive integers

The consequence is a Sturmian itinerary: the sequence of branch choices has exactly p(k) = k + 1 distinct blocks of every length k, the smallest complexity a nonrepeating sequence can have. Its topological entropy is zero. And this holds for every starting integer ≥ 2.

Never repeating, yet precisely predictable

Branch 2 multiplies the number by 9/2 and branch 3 by 16/3. Weight those by their exact frequencies and the binary logarithm of the number grows at an exact rate of γ = θ·log2(9/2) + (1 − θ)·log2(16/3) ≈ 2.2747033 bits per step. More striking is the remainder. Measured from the state at the moment T when the orbit enters the rotation regime, the deviation from that straight line stays strictly below

| log2(xT+n / xT) − γn | < log2(32/27) ≈ 0.2451

for every n ≥ 0, no matter how many steps follow. A sequence that never becomes periodic nonetheless has a growth law with an error below a quarter of a bit.

Source

Autonomous Prime-Valuation Dynamics and Symbolic Complexity in the UNNS Substrate, v1.2. The uniform deviation bound is stated there as Theorem 6.1(iii) and is written as an explicit corollary in the extended synthesis.

Section 4

When Periodic Processes Produce Aperiodicity

MAXPP is one way for arithmetic to avoid repeating itself. A second, built quite differently, shows that aperiodicity does not require anything exotic. It can emerge from pieces that are individually periodic.

The ADJ4 selector follows a fixed ordered list of 22 admissible moves and always plays the first one that applies. For the designated seed 24·36·54·73, the orbit passes through three interacting regimes, each locally orderly:

  • Regime I
    a period-45 block converts exponents of 5 into exponents of 7 (49 → 151)
  • Regime II
    a period-11 register cycle converts 2 units of 7 into 38 of 2
  • Regime III
    a run of branch 4 turns exponents of 2 back into exponents of 5

Composing the three exchange rates gives the leading factor of the system’s selected return:

(151/49) · (38/2) · (2/2) = 2869/49 ≈ 58.551
return law   z′ = (2869 z + δa,r) / 49   with a finite residue-correction table

The expansion of the return parameter is the heart of the mechanism. The conclusion that the global itinerary is not eventually periodic comes from the complete certified return analysis, including the residue corrections and the changing lengths of the regimes, even though every local block is periodic.

An honest status note

The multiplier 2869/49 follows from transparent bookkeeping of prime exponents. The finite correction table and the non-periodicity theorem rest on a computer-assisted exact certificate: 196 symbolic families and 99,866 exact obligations, checked by a separate implementation. It is an internally audited result, not a theorem of computational universality. Source: Autonomous Aperiodicity in the UNNS Substrate, v2.7.

Section 5 · The centerpiece

From Divisors to Entropy: One Exact Correspondence

Everything so far studied one path through the structure, or the structure itself. Now consider every path at once. Take an integer x. For each divisor N ≥ 2 of x, the graph contains a four-cycle through the scaling edge x — 4x. Gather them all, sharing that one edge, and you have the arithmetic book graph F(x): two hub vertices, a direct edge between them, and τ(x) − 1 alternative paths of length three. For x = 8 it is the book of three pages shown below.

Now count histories that never immediately retrace a step. The growth rate of those histories, their entropy h, is the logarithm of the unique real root λ of a cubic equation. With m = τ(x) − 1 pages:

λ³ − λ² + λ = m,   h = ln λ

And here the three descriptions lock together.

τ(x) − 1  =  β1(F(x))  =  e3h − e2h + eh
Arithmetic · Topology · Symbolic dynamics
Left side

Count the positive divisors of x and subtract one. This is arithmetic.

Middle term

The number of independent cycles of the graph built from x. This is topology.

Right side

The entropy of nonbacktracking histories on that graph, written back as a cubic in eh. This is dynamics.

The proof is short, which is part of the surprise. A connected graph has cycle rank |E| − |V| + 1. The book graph has 2m + 2 vertices and 3m + 1 edges, so its cycle rank is m. And the entropy theorem says exactly that λ = eh satisfies the cubic with right-hand side m. The chain closes: an arithmetic invariant determines a topological invariant, which determines a dynamical one.

A concrete case: 64

The number 64 = 26 has seven divisors. Its book graph has six pages and therefore six independent cycles, and the cubic has the exact root λ = 2, because 8 − 4 + 2 = 6.

Scope

This is not an identity for arbitrary graphs. It is an exact correspondence for one explicitly constructed family, and it uses the cycle rank of the graph F(x) itself. It sits beside, but is not asserted to coincide with, the finite-history topology studied in the wider GIG programme. Section 6

The Unexpected Constants of UNNS

Four results stand out as exact constants, and each is worth understanding as the answer to a precise question. They are derived, not fitted. Their significance lies in the laws that determine them, and none is offered as a universal physical constant.

Geometry

6 · 5 · 5

The exact maximal graph distances between consecutive integers, an integer and its double, and consecutive squares. They measure how close arithmetic neighbours can be in the reversible graph.

Rotation

0.572529…

The irrational number ln 48 / ln 864. It is the frequency of branch 2 in an autonomous, nonrepeating selection and fixes the exact growth rate of the number.

Expansion

2869/49

The leading factor of a certified return mechanism, assembled from three exponent-exchange rates. It makes the global orbit expand and refuse to repeat.

Entropy

1/3

The exact coefficient in the growth of nonbacktracking entropy for the book-graph family: h grows like ⅓ ln m, squeezed between ⅓ ln m and ⅓ ln m + 1/(3m1/3).

Finite windows, unbounded history

The entropy family has a further consequence. Because the book graphs sit inside the integer graph as genuine finite subgraphs, the supremum of nonbacktracking entropy over finite subgraphs is infinite. Even for a window of integers 1, …, X, the entropy is guaranteed to be at least

hNB(G[1, X]) ≥ ⅓ ln ln X − C

for a constant C and large X. What has been proved is a lower bound, and the bound grows doubly logarithmically, slowly but without limit. The actual entropy of the windows may grow faster, and the computed values below indicate that it does.

The same spectral analysis reveals a counterweight. As the books grow and the entropy rises, the relative spectral gap eventually closes like ½m−1/3 (it peaks at m = 6 and then decreases), and the share of the maximal-entropy walk spent on the hub edge falls like ⅓m−1/3. More possible histories coexist with a slower relaxation. (The underlying transition structure has period two, so the relaxation statement concerns the two-step chain.)

Section 7

What These Discoveries Change

None of this replaces established mathematics. It enters it, at precise points, and contributes a particular arithmetic realization and the relationships proved inside it.

With that map in view, the sharpest conceptual lesson of the programme can be stated.

Selected order: one history

MAXPP chooses exactly one branch from each state. Its itinerary is deterministic, nonperiodic, Sturmian, with p(k) = k + 1 and zero topological entropy.

Admissible history: all histories

The reversible graph allows every continuation except immediate reversal. Its finite subsystems have entropy ln λm, unbounded as m grows, so Hfin(G) = +∞.

The apparent paradox disappears once a selected history is distinguished from all structurally admissible histories. Complexity is not one universal quantity. It depends on what we measure, which rules we permit, and which structure we examine. Aperiodicity is not chaos; observed complexity is not generated complexity; and the number of admissible histories is not the complexity of the rule that picks one.

Where it touches established theory

Number theory

Prime valuations

The valuation law turns multiplication into addition. A trajectory has finite prime support exactly when it uses finitely many branch labels, by a classical finiteness theorem of Størmer on consecutive smooth numbers.

Symbolic dynamics

Irrational rotations

MAXPP lands in the classical world of Sturmian words and minimal aperiodic complexity (Morse and Hedlund). The UNNS contribution is deriving it from a simple arithmetic selector, with an exact growth law.

Spectral graph theory

Ihara–Bass and entropy

The nonbacktracking matrix of the book graphs brings in Ihara zeta functions and the maximal-entropy Markov measure, here with an explicit Perron root, full spectrum and periodic-orbit counts.

Topology and information

Cycle rank and entropy

The correspondence in Section 5 relates a graph’s first Betti number to the information growth of its histories, in one explicit family.

Section 8

Beyond the Four Papers

The UNNS Substrate now possesses more than a collection of interesting arithmetic observations.

It has exact metric laws, autonomous aperiodic dynamics, expanding return mechanisms, and a rigorous connection between arithmetic, topology, and entropy. These discoveries demonstrate how a simple generative foundation can give rise to several mathematically distinct forms of structure, each valid in its own setting and none a restatement of another.

The four manuscripts sit within a wider programme. SELECT studies how selection rules operate on the substrate, FIELD studies collective dynamics, and GIG studies finite histories and their topology. These are distinct but coordinated directions; the arithmetic-dynamics results here do not assume that any one of them is a special case of another. And nothing in these papers identifies a constant with a measured physical quantity. The achievement is mathematical, and it stands without such a claim.

The next question reaches beyond any one construction: which properties of these structures survive when the rules of generation change, and which relationships can be established across different mathematical and physical systems? The foundations for investigating that question are now substantially stronger.
