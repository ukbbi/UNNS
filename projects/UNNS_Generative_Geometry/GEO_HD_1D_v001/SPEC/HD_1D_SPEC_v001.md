GEO-HD-1D — Generative Histories and Global Route Geometry
The aim of HD-1D is to discover the geometry formed by different generative routes, including routes that branch, recombine and reach the same numerical state while carrying different structural histories.

Unlike HD-1B, its main objects are not pairs of occurrence fibres. They are complete routes, their intersections, their information histories and the higher-dimensional structures formed by admissible route rearrangements.

There is already a promising exact starting result.
A generative family with arbitrarily high-dimensional route structure
For every integer \(r\ge1\), the SELECT-II graph contains at least
\[
\boxed{\binom{2r}{r}}
\]

distinct complete histories ending at \(72^r\).

To see why, begin at 1, apply label 1 exactly \(r\) times to reach \(4^r\), then apply another \(r\) labels 1 and \(r\) labels 2 in any order. All such orders are admissible, and all arrive at \(72^r\).

Moreover, choosing independently between the adjacent label pairs \(12\) and \(21\) produces an \(r\)-dimensional combinatorial cube of histories with the same endpoint.

1. Central research question
The numerical state generated by UNNS does not necessarily reveal the route by which it was reached.

Several distinct admissible histories may lead to the same endpoint, even though those histories pass through different intermediate states, occurrence fibres and geometrical configurations.

The governing question is:
What mathematical geometry is formed by the admissible histories of UNNS generation, and what structural information survives, disappears or becomes inaccessible when different histories merge into the same numerical endpoint?

The investigation must establish laws concerning:
1. Branching and recombination of generative routes.
2. Arithmetic conditions for route interchange and convergence.
3. History multiplicity and its growth.
4. Higher-dimensional combinatorial structures formed by histories.
5. Structural information retained by histories but absent from their endpoints.
6. Obstructions to route interchange, equivalence and common futures.
7. The relationship between local occurrence geometry and global history geometry.
The programme is not restricted to displaying paths as curves in Euclidean 3D or 4D space.

Any new geometric structure must arise from explicitly defined mathematical relations.

2. Frozen mathematical foundation
2.1 SELECT-II generation
For positive integers, the labelled SELECT-II transition is

[
m\xrightarrow{N}T_N(m)
=\frac{m}{N}(N+1)^2,
\qquad N\mid m.
]

Each transition is exact and strictly increasing:

[
T_N(m)\ge4m.
]

The system therefore defines a directed acyclic graph of integer states.

Every state has finitely many immediate successors and predecessors.
2.2 Valuation-space representation
For any prime (p),

2v_p(N+1)-v_p(N).
]

Consequently the valuation displacement of a complete history is the sum of its individual transition displacements.

However, this sum telescopes to the difference between the endpoint and initial-state valuations.

Thus total valuation displacement alone cannot distinguish histories sharing the same source and endpoint.

This limitation is inherited arithmetic, not an unresolved conjecture.
2.3 Occurrence fibres
For an integer (m), let

[
s(m)=\prod_p p^{\lfloor v_p(m)/2\rfloor}.
]

Define the occurrence-index fibre

[
D(m)={d\ge2\mid s(m)}.
]

The GEO-I exponent-fibre and Graver-cell constructions remain available for each numerical state.

HD-1D investigates how these state-wise structures are organized along histories.
2.4 Source fidelity
The following distinctions are mandatory:
- SELECT-II individual generation is not FIELD-I collective evolution.
- An occurrence index is not automatically a conserved object under SELECT-II.
- Two paths with equal endpoints are not necessarily equivalent histories.
- A path-length or time coordinate does not automatically encode history.
- Equal total valuation displacement does not imply equal intermediate geometry.
- A combinatorial higher-dimensional cube is not automatically a Euclidean embedded cube.
No additional interaction operator is assumed without an explicit definition.

3. History space and endpoint projection
3.1 Admissible histories
For a fixed starting state (m_0), an admissible history is

[
h=(N_1,\ldots,N_\ell)
]

such that

[
m_j=T_{N_j}(m_{j-1})
]

is valid for every (j=1,\ldots,\ell).

The history includes its initial state and its complete label sequence.

Write

[
\mathcal H(m_0)
]

for all finite histories starting at (m_0).

Histories are ordered by prefix extension.
3.2 Endpoint map
Define

[
\epsilon:\mathcal H(m_0)\to\mathbb N_{>0},
\qquad
\epsilon(h)=m_\ell.
]

The endpoint map need not be injective.

The set

[
\epsilon^{-1}(t)
]

contains all histories from the fixed initial state that reach (t).

For complete histories, require the initial state to be squarefree and have no incoming SELECT-II edge.

When counting histories from all possible complete sources, write

[
\mathcal H_{\mathrm{root}}(t).
]

Do not conflate the fixed-source history set with the union of squarefree-rooted histories.
3.3 History-unfolded geometry
The history-unfolded space retains every admissible prefix as a separate object.

Its projection to the numerical-state graph identifies prefixes with equal endpoints.

This produces a precise distinction:

[
\text{history identity}
\ne
\text{endpoint identity}.
]

The geometry of this projection—particularly the structures it collapses—is a central subject of HD-1D.

4. Primary benchmark: the four histories of 72
The state 72 has four complete squarefree-rooted histories:

[
\begin{aligned}
h_1:&\quad1\to4\to16\to72,\
h_2:&\quad1\to4\to18\to72,\
h_3:&\quad3\to16\to72,\
h_4:&\quad10\to72.
\end{aligned}
]

Their numerical endpoint is identical, but their source states, path lengths and intermediate structures differ.

The two histories beginning at 1 provide the principal equal-root comparison.
4.1 Structural distinction
Along (h_1),

[
D(4)={2},\qquad
D(16)={2,4},
]

while along (h_2),

[
D(4)={2},\qquad
D(18)={3}.
]

Both eventually reach

[
D(72)={2,3,6}.
]

Therefore the occurrence index 2 has different histories of existence:

[
\rho_2(h_1)=(0,1,1,1),
]

[
\rho_2(h_2)=(0,1,0,1).
]

In the first history, index 2 persists through the intermediate state.

In the second, it disappears and subsequently reappears.

This is an exact example of information retained by the history but not by the common endpoint.

The goal is to generalize such distinctions into mathematical results, not merely enumerate them.

5. First theorem programme — Route interchange and arithmetic diamonds
5.1 Admissible interchange
Consider two labels (A,B) from a common state (m).

The two routes are

[
m\xrightarrow{A}T_A(m)
\xrightarrow{B}t
]

and

[
m\xrightarrow{B}T_B(m)
\xrightarrow{A}t.
]

When both orders are admissible, their endpoints coincide because
T_A(T_B(m))
m\frac{(A+1)^2(B+1)^2}{AB}.
]

The important question is therefore not endpoint equality, but when both orders are admissible.
5.2 Theorem target D-T1 — Arithmetic diamond criterion
Derive a complete necessary-and-sufficient divisibility criterion for both ordered routes to exist.

For each prime (p), write

[
a_p=v_p(A),\quad
b_p=v_p(B),\quad
u_p=v_p(m).
]

Both orders are admissible exactly when

[
u_p\ge a_p,\qquad u_p\ge b_p,
]

and

[
u_p+2v_p(A+1)-a_p\ge b_p,
]

[
u_p+2v_p(B+1)-b_p\ge a_p
]

for every prime.

Prove and implement this exact criterion.

Classify:
- Admissible two-route diamonds.
- One-way-only label orderings.
- Cases where both labels are initially possible but only one ordering is complete.
- Minimal starting states permitting a specified interchange.
- Higher-order compatibility among several label interchanges.
The elementary criterion is the foundation for later general results.
5.3 Route equivalence
Define an elementary route interchange as replacing adjacent admissible labels

[
AB\longleftrightarrow BA
]

while preserving the starting state, ending state and admissibility of the entire history.

Let (\sim_{\mathrm{swap}}) denote the equivalence relation generated by such interchanges.

The distinction

[
h\sim_{\mathrm{swap}}h'
]

versus

[
\epsilon(h)=\epsilon(h')
]

must be maintained.

The second is weaker.

Research question: What arithmetic features determine whether two histories with equal endpoints are related by admissible swaps?

6. Second theorem programme — Intrinsic route multiplicity
6.1 Complete-history count
Define

[
H(t)=|\mathcal H_{\mathrm{root}}(t)|.
]

If (t) is squarefree, then

[
H(t)=1.
]

For a nonsquarefree state, every immediate predecessor is determined by a nontrivial square divisor (d^2\mid t):

[
m=\frac{t(d-1)}{d^2}.
]

Consequently

[
\boxed{
H(t)=
\sum_{\substack{d\ge2\d^2\mid t}}
H\left(\frac{t(d-1)}{d^2}\right).
}
]

This recurrence follows directly from SELECT-II's predecessor characterization.

It provides an exact arithmetic history-counting method without enumerating the entire forward graph.

It is a basic theorem, not by itself a claim of mathematical novelty.
6.2 Theorem D-T2 — Combinatorial growth of histories
A stronger generative phenomenon occurs in the family

[
t_r=72^r.
]

Proposed theorem:

[
\boxed{H(72^r)\ge\binom{2r}{r},\qquad r\ge1.}
]
Constructive proof
Begin at 1 and apply label 1 exactly (r) times:

[
1\to4\to4^2\to\cdots\to4^r.
]

Starting from (4^r=2^{2r}), apply exactly (r) additional labels 1 and (r) labels 2 in any order.

Every ordering is admissible. Each label 1 multiplies the current state by 4, whereas label 2 multiplies it by (9/2). The initial exponent (2r) of prime 2 guarantees that all (r) applications of label 2 are possible, regardless of their order.

Every ordering ends at

[
4^r\cdot4^r\cdot(9/2)^r=72^r.
]

There are

[
\binom{2r}{r}
]

distinct orderings, hence at least that many distinct complete histories.

This proves the lower bound.
Research extension
Determine:
- Stronger bounds or exact formulas for (H(72^r)).
- Growth rates for other endpoint families.
- Structural restrictions on multiplicity.
- Whether endpoint arithmetic predicts the number of interchange-equivalence classes.
- How much multiplicity arises from commuting labels versus genuinely different generative constructions.
The distinction between total history count and swap-equivalence-class count is central.

7. Third theorem programme — Higher-dimensional history cubes
The preceding construction produces more than many histories.

It produces an explicit family of higher-dimensional combinatorial structures.
7.1 Commuting interchange cells
Whenever two independent adjacent swaps can both be performed, their four combinations form a combinatorial square.

When three independent swaps are available, their eight combinations form a combinatorial cube.

Generalize this principle to (d) mutually independent swaps.

Attach a (d)-dimensional combinatorial cube only when all (2^d) histories and all required swap relations are valid.

The construction must use exact admissibility rather than an assumed independence relation.
7.2 Theorem D-T3 — Unbounded history-cube dimension
For every integer (r\ge1), the complete-history space over endpoint (72^r) contains a combinatorial (r)-cube of mutually interchangeable histories.
Construction
Use the initial prefix

[
1\xrightarrow{1}\cdots\xrightarrow{1}4^r.
]

Continue with (r) adjacent blocks, each independently chosen as

[
12\quad\text{or}\quad21.
]

All (2^r) choices are admissible and share endpoint (72^r).

Changing one block corresponds to one elementary swap.

Independent block changes commute, providing the face relations of an (r)-dimensional combinatorial cube.

Therefore the route-recombination structure contains combinatorial cubes of unbounded dimension.
Required distinction
This theorem concerns the combinatorial history complex.

It does not assert that the exponent-coordinate fibre of (72^r) has Euclidean dimension (r), or that these cubes admit a particular metric embedding.

It identifies a different and intrinsically generative source of higher-dimensional organization.
7.3 Further theorem targets
Investigate:
- Maximum cubical dimension for a fixed endpoint.
- The topology of finite history-recombination complexes.
- Connected components under admissible swaps.
- Minimal obstructions to completing a square or higher cube.
- Whether local admissibility of individual swaps guarantees global higher-cube compatibility.
- The relation between combinatorial cube dimension, history multiplicity and occurrence complexity.
A dimension count alone is insufficient. The objective is to derive structural laws governing the route complex.

8. Fourth theorem programme — Causal occurrence histories
8.1 Why the earlier partial transport map is inadequate
HD-0 considered identity restrictions between occurrence fibres along SELECT-II edges.

Its complete-history version was vacuous: every complete source-rooted history begins at a squarefree state with empty occurrence fibre.

Therefore transporting only indices present at the initial root produces an empty map.

That no-go result remains valid.

HD-1D must introduce an explicitly defined history-sensitive structure that records later births, losses and reappearances.

Do not silently reuse the failed transport definition.
8.2 Occurrence event system
For a history

[
h\to m_1\to\cdots\to m_\ell,
]

define its birth and loss sets at step (j):

[
B_j=D(m_j)\setminus D(m_{j-1}),
]

[
L_j=D(m_{j-1})\setminus D(m_j).
]

For any occurrence index (d), define its temporal incidence signature

[
\rho_d(h)=
\big(
\mathbf1_{d\in D(m_0)},\ldots,
\mathbf1_{d\in D(m_\ell)}
\big).
]

These signatures determine the index's periods of presence and absence along the history.

They remain meaningful even when the initial fibre is empty.
8.3 Theorem target D-T4 — Non-reconstructibility of history from endpoints
Prove general conditions under which histories with the same source and endpoint must have different intermediate occurrence signatures.

The SELECT-II diamond

[
m\to4m\to18m,
]

[
m\to\frac{9m}{2}\to18m
]

exists for every even (m).

The intermediate occurrence fibres differ, because their prime-2 square-root exponents differ.

This supplies an infinite family of route pairs with equal source and endpoint but different intermediate occurrence structure.

Develop this elementary fact into general theorems concerning:
- Which occurrence-index histories are forced to differ.
- Which labels cause loss or reappearance.
- Which event sequences can arise under admissible rearrangements.
- Whether a history signature distinguishes any classes of non-swap-equivalent histories.
- Which aspects of occurrence history are preserved under route interchange.
Avoid defining a new invariant that merely restates the full sequence of labels.

The mathematical goal is to identify smaller, structurally meaningful information that cannot be recovered from the endpoint alone.
8.4 Geometric-history extension
For each intermediate state, attach its GEO-I occurrence geometry.

Study sequences

[
U(m_0),U(m_1),\ldots,U(m_\ell)
]

together with the exact birth/loss events and any explicitly defined inter-fibre geometric maps.

Possible objects include:
- History-indexed occurrence incidences.
- Geometric survival intervals.
- Reappearance classes.
- Route-dependent changes of abstract and realized topology.
- Relations among occurrence geometry, route swapping and recombination.
Do not assume monotone geometric preservation along SELECT-II histories.

9. Fifth theorem programme — Global merging and common futures
9.1 Merging
Two histories merge when their endpoints coincide.

Classify merging into:
1. Different routes from the same source.
2. Routes from different squarefree sources.
3. Mergers explained by adjacent label swaps.
4. Mergers not explained by such swaps.
5. Mergers with different occurrence-event signatures.
6. Mergers with different intermediate geometric structures.
The four complete histories of 72 are the initial deterministic benchmark.
9.2 Common futures
For states (a,b), define their common-future set as

[
J(a,b)=
\operatorname{Reach}^{+}(a)
\cap
\operatorname{Reach}^{+}(b),
]

where (\operatorname{Reach}^{+}) includes the chosen zero-step or positive-step convention explicitly.

The SELECT-II baseline already establishes that universal joins do not exist.

Consequently the programme must not assume every pair of branches has a common future.
9.3 Theorem target D-T5 — Arithmetic recombination criterion
Seek an exact arithmetic criterion for common futures or for a meaningful restricted class of route recombinations.

Possible outcomes include:
- A general sufficient criterion.
- A necessary obstruction.
- A classification for a specified family.
- An infinite class with no common future.
- A bounded minimal-common-successor theorem with a proof extending beyond a finite search.
The earlier 96/27 bounded common-successor experiments may serve as regression data, but a bounded enumeration is not a universal theorem.
9.4 Global route obstruction
Define obstructions to:
- Completing a two-step diamond.
- Rearranging a history by admissible swaps.
- Connecting two distinct histories within a fixed-endpoint swap complex.
- Finding a common future.
- Preserving a selected structural signature across a merge.
These obstruction types must remain distinct.

A missing swap does not imply a missing common future, and an absent common future is stronger than a failure of local commutation.

13. Computational and verification requirements
The package must use exact integer arithmetic.

Construct histories in a deterministic manner, with labels and starting states retained.

For finite endpoints, use the predecessor recurrence where possible rather than expensive unrestricted forward searches.

The exact verifier must check:
- Every transition label divides its source state.
- Every endpoint is computed correctly.
- History counts satisfy the predecessor recurrence.
- Swap faces consist entirely of admissible histories.
- Higher cubes have all required vertices and faces.
- Occurrence-event records agree with direct fibre computation.
- Proposed counterexamples violate precisely the stated hypotheses.
- Bounded searches are not represented as infinite proofs.
The Windows runner must support individually executable stages below the observed local shell-duration limit.

The builder and verification logic should be separated sufficiently to detect ordinary implementation errors.

This is a mathematical reproducibility requirement, not a programme of external review.

GEO-HD-1 C3 candidate
