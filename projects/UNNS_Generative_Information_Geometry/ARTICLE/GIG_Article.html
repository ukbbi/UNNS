The Hidden Geometry
of Generation

What if the deepest secrets of a mathematical result lie in the histories it conceals?

One outcome can emerge from many different paths. Some histories connect, others are forbidden, and some form unexpected topological structures that counting alone cannot reveal.

The UNNS Substrate uncovers this hidden architecture—showing how arithmetic determines which histories are possible, why certain topological holes survive, and how even forbidden histories can reveal the geometry of what is allowed.

Overview

This article is the public companion to the research monograph Generative Information Geometry in the UNNS Substrate: Ancestry, Admissibility, Topology and Information Recoverability (UNNS Research Collective, Version 2.0): a 60-page mathematical treatise, developed through fifteen research releases.

Its question is simple to state: how much mathematical information does a generative history contain beyond its final state? The answer leads from counting histories to an arithmetic geometry of admissibility, exact conditions for the survival of topological obstructions, a constructive route to higher-dimensional homology, a precise separation between what an observer can and cannot recover, and a duality in which the histories that are forbidden describe the topology of those that are allowed.

A generated result contains less information than the structure of the histories capable of producing it. UNNS now provides an exact mathematical way to study that difference.

1A number is not the whole history of its generation

A number tells us what has been obtained. It does not necessarily tell us how that result was generated.

Two processes may reach the same endpoint through entirely different sequences of operations. Some histories can be transformed into one another by exchanging admissible steps. Others cannot. Some collections of histories form loops. Others enclose higher-dimensional topological obstructions. And the histories that are forbidden can contain enough mathematical information to reconstruct the topology of those that are allowed.

The UNNS Substrate studies mathematical structures through the operations and admissibility conditions by which they arise. One particular arithmetic grammar, SELECT-II, uses the transformation

TN(m) = m · (N + 1)² / N,   N | ma step is allowed only when N divides the current state m

A history is a sequence of such transformations, each satisfying the divisibility requirement at the moment it is applied. Consider the endpoint 72. Under this grammar it has exactly four complete histories, written (source; operations):

(1; 1, 2, 1)1 → 4 → 18 → 72
(1; 1, 1, 2)1 → 4 → 16 → 72
(3; 3, 2)3 → 16 → 72
(10; 5)10 → 72

The histories are distinct although their final value is identical. Two originate from 1 and differ in the ordering of their operations; the other two arise from different sources. If only the endpoint is observed, its ancestry cannot be reconstructed.

For the finite set Ft of complete histories of t, define the ancestral ambiguity

A(t) = log₂ |Ft|    so    A(72) = log₂ 4 = 2 bits

But those two bits conceal different kinds of uncertainty: one part concerns the source, another the operations applied, another which sequences can be rearranged into one another. The monograph develops an exact decomposition into source, generating content, swap class and individual-history information. It uses the established Shannon and Hartley measures; its contribution is to identify and organise the generative structures to which those measures apply.

The count explodes, and one bound is exact

Take the endpoints 72, 72², 72³ and so on. Their history counts grow rapidly, and the monograph proves that |F72r| is at least the central binomial coefficient C(2r, r) for every r.

First discovery
Information about an endpoint and information about its ancestry are different mathematical objects, and ancestry itself contains more structure than its number of possible histories.

2Arithmetic generates geometry

Fix a source and a collection of distinct generating operations. Different orderings of those operations become different possible histories; some orderings are admissible, others violate the grammar. Now connect two admissible histories whenever one can be obtained from the other by exchanging two neighbouring operations without violating admissibility. A graph appears: its vertices are histories, its edges are legal swaps.

That graph is only the beginning. Independent exchanges generate square faces. Certain three-operation relations generate hexagonal faces. More complicated relations generate cubes, prisms and higher-dimensional cells. The resulting structure is a subcomplex of a permutohedron, the classical polytope whose vertices represent permutations.

What makes the UNNS realisation distinctive is the arithmetic selection of vertices and faces. The monograph proves that admissibility is controlled by the divisibility requirements attached to the prefix sets of a history. Every history has an arithmetic realisation threshold, and so does every permutohedral face F:

F ∈ XC(m)  ⟺  KF | mXC(m) is the history complex for content C and source m; KF is the face's arithmetic threshold

As the source changes, arithmetic therefore decides which geometric structures become available. When m divides m′, the complexes are nested:

m | m′  ⟹  XC(m) ⊆ XC(m′)
Second discovery
Arithmetic admissibility does more than permit or forbid calculations: it organises a space of histories with a mathematically determined topology.

Arithmetic Generating Geometry: What Was Known, and What UNNS Adds

The relationship between arithmetic and geometry has a long mathematical history. Number-theoretic constraints have long been used to construct and classify geometric objects. Permutohedra represent the geometry of permutations. Arithmetic matroids and toric arrangements connect discrete arithmetic information with combinatorial geometry. In combinatorial commutative algebra, cellular resolutions associate arithmetic labels with geometric cells, allowing divisibility conditions to select subcomplexes. These developments establish an important precedent: arithmetic can organise, constrain and filter geometric structures.

The UNNS Substrate does not claim to have originated that general idea. Its contribution lies in a different starting point and a particular chain of consequences.

From arithmetic operations to realisable geometry

Rather than beginning with a geometric complex and assigning arithmetic labels to it, UNNS begins with a generative rule. In SELECT-II the step TN(m) = m(N+1)²/N is permitted only when N divides the current state m, and a sequence of steps is admissible only when its successive prefixes satisfy the corresponding arithmetic requirements. These conditions determine which complete histories can occur. The admissible histories become the vertices of a complex; legal exchanges of neighbouring operations determine its edges; compatible exchanges determine higher faces. The geometry is selected by the realisability conditions of a specified arithmetic process.

Divisibility-based selection alone has classical precedents. The significance here is that these divisibility conditions arise from the admissibility of successive UNNS operations and govern the geometry of their realisable histories.

From realisability to topological survival

Different arithmetic sources can produce history complexes with different homology. For two distinguished two-dimensional cycles, survival is governed by exact arithmetic laws:

[z₁] ≠ 0 ⟺ gcd(t, 30) = 1  (sources 288t)   ·   [z₂] ≠ 0 ⟺ gcd(t, 42) = 1  (sources 432t)

These conditions do not merely count histories or activate individual cells: they determine whether particular homological obstructions survive. The research also establishes minimal filling-family criteria, constructs higher-dimensional classes, connects the topology of admissible histories to the homology of forbidden-prefix complexes, and shows that equal history counts can conceal different topology.

The distinctive UNNS contribution
Arithmetic generation determines admissibility; admissibility determines history geometry; geometry supports homological obstructions; arithmetic conditions govern their survival; and the resulting structures can be investigated through information-theoretic observations and forbidden-history duality.
The novelty sought in UNNS is not that numbers can describe geometry, but that the admissibility of a specified arithmetic generation process determines a geometry of possible histories, and permits exact mathematical results about its topology and recoverability.

The individual tools have established predecessors. Determining the full extent of the originality of the specific SELECT-II constructions, survival results, certified higher-dimensional examples and their integration into a theory of generative-history information relative to all prior literature remains a matter for independent specialist assessment. What is established within the stated grammars is this: a concrete arithmetic generative process can determine not only which histories exist, but also the topology those histories form, the obstructions they support, and the information needed to distinguish their structures.

3Some histories surround holes, and arithmetic decides whether those holes survive

In topology a hole is not necessarily an empty region of ordinary space. It may be a loop that cannot be contracted, a closed two-dimensional cycle that does not bound a three-dimensional chain, or a higher-dimensional analogue. The UNNS history complexes exhibit such structures. One central result is a two-dimensional cycle consisting of 96 faces: 64 squares and 32 hexagons.

For the content C₁ = (2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9), at sources m = 288t, the distinguished homology class survives precisely when gcd(t, 30) = 1. For C₂ = (2, 3, 6, 7, 8, 9), at sources m = 432t, the condition is gcd(t, 42) = 1. These are exact survival laws for the specified anchor constructions.

The research goes further. For the 96-face cycle the exact filling criterion reduces to four minimal families of admissible prefix sets, B₀ to B₃. The cycle survives when it is present and none of these families becomes fully available; if one does, the cycle can be filled. Of the 540 three-dimensional faces of the six-label permutohedron, exactly six obstruct this cycle's certificate, and the criterion is exact: 589,839 of the 2²⁰ prefix families bound it. More generally, every cycle in the studied permutohedral setting has its own minimal filling-family criterion; the four families and the gcd laws belong to the anchors, while the general principle is survival through minimal admissible fillings.

An important distinction

A topological obstruction is not explained merely by noticing that a geometric face is missing. Its survival depends on whether the admissible structure contains an appropriate filling chain.

Third discovery
An exact arithmetic mechanism governs topological survival.

4Higher-dimensional topology can be constructed, not merely detected

The research does not stop at identifying existing holes. It constructs relationships between homology in different dimensions. A lifting operator Λ satisfies the chain identity

∂Λ(f) = Λ(∂f) + first(f) + last(f)over the field 𝔽₂

which lets lower-dimensional cycles and their fillings take part in constructions one dimension higher. The distinction between alternative fillings is decisive: pairing a filling with itself produces a lift that bounds; different fillings can produce a lifted cycle representing a nontrivial higher-dimensional class. The lifted class depends on the choice of filling exactly through a first-facet complex.

The programme developed this through explicit calculations and certified examples of nontrivial third-dimensional homology, and a certified four-dimensional hole: the eight-label content (2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9, 15, 20) at source m = 86400, with a 2,146-face cycle, together with seven further verified neighbouring contents. It is a single bounded example, not a census.

What is, and is not, claimed

The results do not assert that every lower-dimensional hole generates a nontrivial higher-dimensional one. They establish the lifting calculus, its exact boundary relations, and concrete nontrivial constructions. General-dimension statements are limited to the chain-level calculus; everything that names a content is a finite or bounded computation.

Fourth discovery
A constructive route from admissible generation to higher-dimensional topology.

5Equal information does not mean equal structure

If two systems admit the same number of histories they have the same Hartley ambiguity. Does that mean they have the same generative structure? No. The research provides explicit arithmetic systems with equal history counts but different topology. Take the content C = (2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 15). At sources 480 and 800 both systems contain exactly 396 admissible orderings, yet

β₂(XC(480)) = 1   ,   β₂(XC(800)) = 0

The first has a nontrivial two-dimensional homology class; the second does not. A count of histories alone cannot tell them apart.

The investigation also constructed pairs whose accessibility information agrees through two legal swaps while their first Betti numbers differ; there, differences in hexagonal two-cells become detectable only at a greater observational radius. The monograph organises this into a ladder of observations, from bare counts through local swap accessibility and graph structure to complete admissibility data.

That census result shows why care is needed. A statistic that works perfectly on one finite family need not work on a larger one. The research produced explicit counterexamples and recorded a failed pre-registered prediction.

That outcome is scientifically valuable: it distinguishes information that merely describes a known dataset from information that genuinely supports reconstruction beyond it.

Fifth discovery
A rigorous separation between generative ambiguity, local accessibility and global topology.

6The surprising duality: forbidden histories reveal permitted topology

Perhaps the most revealing result is that the topology of admissible generation can be studied through the histories that are forbidden. Consider all orderings of six distinguishable events. Some are admissible under the chosen grammar, others are not. The admissible histories generate a complex KA; the forbidden histories determine a complementary structure YA. By Alexander duality, in the six-event setting,

β₂(KA) = dim H₁(YA; 𝔽₂)for every admissible set A with ∅ ≠ A ⊊ S₆

A two-dimensional homological obstruction in admissible history space therefore corresponds to a first-homology class in the forbidden-history complex. For prefix-defined grammars the construction simplifies further. A dead prefix set is a partial set of operations through which no complete admissible ordering passes; the compatible intersections of dead sets form a smaller simplicial complex, the dead-set nerve, and

β₂(KA) = dim H₁(N[DA]; 𝔽₂)six events, prefix-defined A

Instead of building the whole admissible complex and searching it for a two-dimensional hole, one can examine the relationships among forbidden prefixes. In certified examples a nonbounding cycle in the dead-set nerve identifies the admissible obstruction. Homotopy-preserving reductions often compress the nerve dramatically: in the six-label census, 105 of the 151 distinct positive dead-set patterns reduce to a bare three-vertex hollow triangle.

A pair that counts cannot separate

For the content (2, 4, 5, 9, 14, 15), sources 2520 and 3780 both admit 540 histories and both have five dead sets. At 2520 the dead sets form a cycle that does not bound, so β₂ = 1; at 3780 they do not, so β₂ = 0. The difference lies not in how many forbidden sets there are, but in how they fit together.

This does not turn every forbidden pattern into a hole: a visible cycle may still bound through other cells. The decisive property is homological.

Sixth discovery
The topology of what can be generated may be reconstructed from the relationships among what cannot be generated.

7Beyond integers: the grammar itself controls possible topology

The early experiments used the arithmetic of SELECT-II, but the UNNS Substrate is not restricted to integer-valued states. This raises a crucial question: are the observed topological structures peculiar to integer divisibility, or can they arise from more general generative grammars? The research investigated symbolic systems in which events are distinguishable and permitted orderings are constrained by local transition rules.

For six events a theorem establishes that imposing admissibility through consecutive-pair constraints cannot produce nontrivial second homology. Yet allowing constraints on consecutive triples changes the situation. A certified example that forbids only two specific consecutive triples has 674 admissible orderings and Betti numbers

(β₀, β₁, β₂, β₃, β₄, β₅) = (1, 2, 5, 0, 0, 0)

so the permitted histories contain five independent second-dimensional homology classes, and no SELECT-II divisibility is needed.

This is a sharp distinction, within the six-event model, between the topological capabilities of two kinds of constraint. The topology available to a generative system depends on the expressive structure of its admissibility grammar. Integer arithmetic is one way to generate that structure, but it is not the only way.

Seventh discovery
Topological complexity can emerge from the locality and organisation of generative constraints themselves.

8How the findings relate to established mathematics

Generative Information Geometry draws on several well-established fields. Its contribution is not to replace their foundations but to connect them through explicitly defined generative systems.

Information theory

Shannon and Hartley

Shannon entropy quantifies uncertainty under a distribution; Hartley information the logarithm of the number of possibilities. GIG uses both without redefining them and asks a structural question: what do the possible histories look like, and what can an observer reconstruct of their organisation? Equal ambiguity can coexist with different topology, so entropy and topology are complementary, not interchangeable.

Topology

Algebraic and combinatorial

Permutohedra, chain complexes, Alexander duality and nerve theorems are classical. GIG places them inside arithmetic and symbolic spaces of admissible histories; the grammar specifies which complexes arise, and classical mathematics supplies the proof techniques.

Persistence

Persistent homology

Divisibility orders the sources, and faces enter the complex as thresholds are met: a filtration controlled by prime valuations rather than a distance parameter. The survival laws give exact information along it. A complete multiparameter persistence analysis is a separate task.

Algebra

Combinatorial commutative algebra

Labelling permutohedral faces by least common multiples resembles cellular resolutions of monomial modules (Bayer–Sturmfels), suggesting an algebraic reading of the obstructions. The monograph identifies the connection; it does not claim to have computed a corresponding resolution.

Dynamics

Symbolic dynamics

A grammar of allowed adjacent transitions resembles the local rules of symbolic dynamics. GIG studies the topology of finite admissible orderings under such constraints; the six-event locality theorem and its triple counterexample show that the span of the constraints changes the attainable homology.

10A different way to think about mathematical information

The familiar question in information theory is how uncertain we are about a value, state or event. Generative Information Geometry introduces another mathematically precise question:

How much of the structure that produced an outcome remains recoverable when its history is compressed into an observation?

That structure may include alternative sources, alternative operation sequences, connected families of histories, noncontractible cycles, higher-dimensional obstructions and entire families of forbidden transitions. A count does not generally reveal all of it. A local graph observation may reveal more but still miss global structure. A forbidden-history nerve may retain exactly the homological feature being sought.

The deeper conclusion is that the geometry of admissible generation contains mathematically distinguishable information beyond a simple description of possible outcomes. This does not invalidate classical information theory; it identifies structured objects whose properties can be studied alongside entropy. What began as a question about the many histories of a generated number has become a rigorous investigation of the arithmetic, geometric and topological structure of generation itself.

Conclusion: from generated values to the geometry of possibility

The principal revelation of Generative Information Geometry is that generation has a mathematical architecture. A final state is one projection of that architecture. Behind it lie histories, permissible rearrangements, arithmetic thresholds, connected components, homological obstructions and forbidden configurations. The UNNS research programme has established exact relationships among these structures in the grammars investigated. Some arise from arithmetic, others from general combinatorial topology, and still others persist in symbolic systems where divisibility plays no role.

The result
The information contained in generation is organised not only by how many histories exist, but by which histories are admissible, how those histories are related, and which forbidden structures constrain them.

The monograph provides the definitions, proofs, exact constructions, certificates and computational records supporting these conclusions. The next mathematical challenges concern broader grammars, dynamical evolution and more general reconstruction principles; they do not diminish what has already been established.
